Help

How to use 'Quantum annealing'

This solver translates binary optimization problems into quantum models (Ising Hamiltonians) and generates Qiskit circuits to solve them via Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA).

1. Define objective

Write the objective function to minimize in the 'Minimize cost function' field. Variables must be of form x0, x1, etc. Example: 12x0 + 14x1 + 16x2 + 3x3.

2. Add constraints & penalties

Add equality constraints in the 'Subject to constraints' area, one per line. Format: expression=value. You can optionally add a specific penalty stiffness using expression=value, lambda. If no lambda is provided, the Global Lambda value will be applied. Example: 7x0+20x1+3x2+2x3=1, 10.

3. QUBO & Ising Mapping

The system expands constraint equations, applies quadratic penalty rules, and maps variables xi ∈ [0, 1] to spin variables si ∈ [-1, +1]. This creates the transverse field Ising Hamiltonian H = ∑ hi Zi + ∑ Jij Zi Zj.

4. Generate Qiskit circuit

Generates full, executable Qiskit code. The circuit includes state preparation (H), cost layer rotations (RZ for linear, RZZ for quadratic interactions), and mixer rotations (RX).

Problem topology graph explained

What is this graph for?

The 'Problem topology graph' is a visual representation of how your mathematical problem maps onto a quantum computer's architecture. It helps you quickly understand which variables (qubits) are interacting with each other, identifying subproblems and heavily constrained variables at a glance.

What do the nodes and lines represent?

  • Nodes (Circles): Each circle represents one of the binary variables from your equations (e.g., x0, x1). In the quantum hardware, these will be assigned to physical Qubits.
  • Edges (Lines): The lines connecting the nodes represent the relationship or dependency between those two variables. If two variables appear together in any of your constraints, they are connected by a line.

What is calculated (Couplings)?

The value hidden in each line is called a Coupling Weight. It represents the physical interaction strength between two qubits. To translate your mathematical constraints into quantum physics, the system squares each equation to create a "penalty function" (so violations increase the quantum energy cost). Squaring a polynomial generates cross-multiplications (quadratic terms) between variables. The universal formula we use to extract this coupling strength from any constraint is:

Coupling = 2 × λ × Coef(A) × Coef(B)
  • λ (Lambda): The penalty weight you assigned to the constraint.
  • Coef(A) & Coef(B): The numbers multiplying the variables inside your equation.
  • 2: Comes from the mathematical expansion of a squared binomial:
    (A + B)2 = A2 + B2 + 2AB

Why do we calculate this?

Quantum annealers (like D-Wave) solve optimization problems by finding the lowest energy state of a physical system. They cannot read your human-written equations natively. They only understand linear biases (applied to single qubits) and quadratic couplings (the physical connections between pairs of qubits). We calculate these couplings so the hardware knows exactly how strongly to link the qubits to accurately reflect your constraints.

Optimization problem explained

What is this section for?

This section allows you to define your mathematical model in a human-readable format. Quantum annealers are specifically designed to solve optimization problems: finding the best possible solution out of many combinations while respecting certain rules.

Cost function

The Cost Function (or objective function) is the equation you want to minimize. For example, if you are planning a delivery route, the variables might represent paths, and the cost function calculates the total distance. The quantum computer will try to find the variable configuration that results in the lowest possible mathematical value.

Constraints

Constraints are the strict rules your solution must follow (e.g., "A truck cannot carry more than 50kg", or "City A must be visited exactly once"). The system will automatically convert these human-readable inequalities or equalities into "quantum penalties".

Lambda (λ)

Lambda is the penalty weight. If the quantum hardware breaks a constraint, we penalize it by adding a huge number to its cost function. Lambda determines how big that penalty is. If Lambda is too low, the hardware might ignore your rules to get a cheaper score. If it's too high, the hardware might only focus on the rules and ignore the actual cost function.

Hardware resource estimation explained

What is this section for?

This section analyzes your problem and estimates the raw hardware capabilities required to map it onto a quantum processing unit (QPU). Since modern quantum hardware has strict physical limitations, estimating these resources beforehand is crucial.

Qubits

Logical Qubits represent the minimum number of quantum bits needed. This is calculated by counting all the unique binary variables (x0, x1, etc.) that appear in your cost function and constraints. Note that in real hardware, due to limited physical connectivity, one "logical" qubit might need to be chained across multiple "physical" qubits (a process called minor-embedding).

Couplings

Couplings are the physical connections required between pairs of qubits. If two variables interact in your mathematical model (by being multiplied together or appearing in the same constraint), a coupling is generated. Hardware with more native connections (like D-Wave's Pegasus architecture) can handle problems with more couplings.

Density

Density is the percentage of possible connections that are actually used. A density of 100% means every single variable interacts with every other variable (a fully connected graph). High density problems are notoriously difficult to embed on real quantum hardware because physical qubits typically only connect to a small neighborhood of adjacent qubits (around 15 connections per qubit on modern chips).

Graph density details

Density measures how interconnected your quantum variables are. A fully connected problem (100% density) means every variable interacts with every other variable. High density graphs are harder to solve on real quantum hardware.
